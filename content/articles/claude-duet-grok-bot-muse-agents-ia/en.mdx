In ten weeks, xAI, Meta, OpenAI and Anthropic all shipped the same thing: an always-on
AI agent that works in the background on its own computer and comes back to you when it
needs a decision. To me, this isn't a new chatbot feature, it's a real change of method:
we stop micromanaging AI, we give it a frame and we judge its work.

Let me say it right away: I haven't tested any of these products. What I have done is
build an agent at work that already does part of what they promise, and that's where I'm
writing from.

## From human in the loop to human on the loop

To place the human relative to agents, I'm using the three positions described by Kief
Morris (Thoughtworks) in
[Humans and Agents in Software Engineering Loops](https://www.martinfowler.com/articles/exploring-gen-ai/humans-and-agents.html),
published in March 2026 on Martin Fowler's site:

- **In the loop**: the human controls things inside the loop, for example by checking
  every generated line, and fixes the result directly. That's micromanagement.
- **On the loop**: the human builds and runs the loop, meaning the harness (specs,
  quality checks, guidance). When the result isn't right, they change the harness that
  produced it. As Morris puts it: "Rather than personally inspecting what the agents
  produce, we can make them better at producing it."
- **Outside the loop**: the human only keeps the "why" and leaves all of the "how" to
  the agents. That's vibe coding.

![Three diagrams: in the loop, the human checks every output of the agent; on the loop, they run the harness around the loop; outside the loop, they only set the goal](/images/articles/claude-duet-grok-bot-muse-agents-ia/loops.svg)

With the first assistants, we were in the loop. We split up the task, guided every step,
reread every line produced. AI was a tool we held by the hand.

With this year's models, we've entered the agentic era, and in the end almost every need
we had can be turned into an agent. The method changes completely:

1. we define the need together;
2. the developer sets the frame;
3. the AI does the rest;
4. the developer checks the result and gives feedback, no longer on the code, but ABOVE
   ALL on the thinking: the analysis and the implementation the AI chose.

That's the shift to human on the loop: the human no longer does everything themselves.
They build the frame, keep the decisions, and take back control at the moments that
matter.

The four products don't put the human in the same place, by the way. Claude Duet keeps
an approval step on sensitive actions (it asks before sending a message or paying).
Grok Bot goes much further: its agents split the work among themselves and only come to
you for judgment calls.

## Blue, my agent at work

I didn't wait for these products. At work, I use an agent I named Blue, which backs me
up on tickets every day. It's actually a whole team of sub-agents:

- **PR Babysitter** watches my pull requests: it checks the content, talks with
  CodeRabbit (the AI code review tool) and gives me its feedback;
- one agent takes simple tickets (and I do mean simple) and handles them from A to Z,
  all the way to opening a draft PR, which I review before anything else happens;
- one agent gives me a briefing in the morning, and another in the evening;
- one agent helps me write documentation, especially to explain the need in more detail;
- one agent pulls information from Slack when a message concerns me.

Let's be clear: Blue doesn't work in my place. Complex tickets, architecture and
decisions are still mine. Blue takes the repetitive work and the exploration, which frees
up time for what really takes thinking.

![Diagram of Blue: Paul sets the harness and reviews; Blue coordinates five sub-agents, PR Babysitter (which talks with CodeRabbit), Tickets, Briefing, Docs and Slack](/images/articles/claude-duet-grok-bot-muse-agents-ia/blue.svg)

Why Blue? Simply because I wanted a color name, and blue is the professional one. Its
personal counterpart, which I'm going to build, will be called Yellow.

I'll be honest: Blue wasn't good on the first try. I had to give it quite a lot of
feedback on how it worked, and what I refined wasn't Blue itself, it was its harness, in
other words the frame it works in. Once the harness is clean, the results follow.

When I started this article, I thought I was behind, still human in the loop. Rereading
these definitions, I realize I've already partly moved past it: when Blue gets it wrong,
I fix its harness, not its code. That's human on the loop, with a final approval step
I'm deliberately keeping (the draft PR).

The next step is the agent swarm: several agents that talk to each other, split the work
and only come back to the human to decide. Grok Bot already does it in group chats, and
all the big labs (Anthropic, OpenAI, xAI, Meta) now have their own always-on agent. It's
a vision I haven't fully grasped yet, and I fully intend to take a closer look, because
I'm convinced it's going to lead to something huge.

### What does a swarm loop look like?

Imagine three agents tackling a bug: one looks for the cause, another proposes a fix, and
a third tries to break it with tests. A coordinator brings their findings together, but
doesn't decide on the human's behalf whether a change affects security or architecture.

![Conceptual diagram of an agent swarm: the human sets the goal and boundaries, a coordinator assigns work to three specialized agents, their results are checked, and sensitive decisions return to the human.](/images/articles/claude-duet-grok-bot-muse-agents-ia/swarm-en.svg)

The number of agents isn't enough. They also need a shared goal, shared workspace,
verifiable outputs and a clear rule for surfacing disagreements. **A conversation between
agents can look coordinated while going in circles.**

### A swarm can cooperate and still fail

So, are we ready for a truly autonomous swarm? In my view, not yet, but we're getting
closer, and fast. An experiment published in October 2026 by Y Combinator with Olam Labs
illustrates this really well
([Agents in Games](https://labs.ycombinator.com/projects/games/index.html)). They set 50
agents loose in Minecraft for 3 hours, with a single goal: "Build a thriving
civilization."

- They exchanged around **5,900 messages**.
- Ada received **616 direct messages**, but she wasn't chosen through a vote or because
  the agents judged her best. Forty-six of the other 49 agents sent their first message
  to her because she was **first in the directory**. The report says there was no vote,
  election or challenge. Ada became the group's hub.
- The colony still never really thrived: **22 of the 34 deaths** happened in the first
  20 minutes.

And that changes how I read the result. Those 616 messages don't prove the agents picked a
good leader; they show how strongly a small setup choice can shape a swarm's hierarchy.
The report's account makes clear there was no deliberation over who should lead.

In the matches pitting a Claude team against a GPT team, we find similar limits:

- the Claude team keeps the same leader despite losing;
- it writes lessons into memory files, then repeats the same mistakes;
- several agents conclude an action is impossible while the GPT team finds a workaround.

> **Knowing how to split up the work isn't the same as learning from failure.**

So, are we ready for a truly autonomous swarm? In my view, not yet, but we're getting
closer, and fast. Swarms already know how to coordinate; they still need to get better at
questioning themselves. That's where the human *on the loop* remains essential: setting
escalation rules and stepping back in when agents repeat a mistake or the stakes exceed
the agreed boundaries.

Today, I see three groups:

- a very small minority of people far ahead, already at the swarm stage, like the labs
  themselves;
- people like me, along with startups, SaaS companies and advanced tech teams, moving
  from human in the loop to human on the loop;
- and lots of developers still in the loop, whom I'm starting to see move with these new
  agents.

Everything is speeding up. Every year brings its own paradigm shift.

### A map of exposure, not a global census

Andrej Karpathy offers another way to look at this gap: four rough orders of magnitude,
based on how much people have encountered large language models (LLMs). He called the
figures *“napkin math”* — back-of-the-envelope estimates, not the result of a global
survey.

![Four rough exposure levels proposed by Andrej Karpathy: about 6 billion people have barely used an LLM, 1 to 2 billion use chatbots occasionally, 20 million use leading models professionally in math or code, and about 5,000 have access to internal lab systems. Estimates, not measured counts.](/images/articles/claude-duet-grok-bot-muse-agents-ia/ai-exposure-en.svg)

- **About 6 billion** have barely come into contact with an LLM. That is not the same as
  “using no AI”: search engines, recommendation systems and spam filters have used AI for
  years.
- **1–2 billion** may use free chatbots now and then, as a better search box or writing
  aid. For many, the experience remains *in the loop*: ask a question, read an answer,
  move on.
- **About 20 million** work with the most capable models, especially in math and code,
  and are beginning to see agents take on work that used to occupy a team for days or
  weeks. This is the group that can really experiment with *on the loop* — though not
  everyone in it has made that shift.
- **About 5,000** people may have access to the most advanced internal systems at the
  labs. That's where people can observe agents collaborating on longer tasks. This does
  not prove that 5,000 people have access to Mythos, or that they are all working on
  swarms.

> These figures mainly describe a **gap in experience**. They do not measure how many
> people “use AI” in the broadest sense, and they cannot count how many developers are
> *in* or *on the loop*.

So “4 billion people don't use AI” mixes two different questions. If we mean all AI, the
definition includes tools most of us never notice. If we mean LLMs, Karpathy's estimate
is closer to **6 billion who have barely encountered them**. Microsoft measures a
different population — the share of working-age people using AI — and put its figure at
**17.8% in Q1 2026**. The definitions and methods differ, so these numbers cannot be
subtracted from one another. ([Karpathy's estimate on X](https://x.com/karpathy/status/2109361546505966046) ·
[Microsoft AI Diffusion indicator](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Microsoft-AI-Diffusion-Report-2026-Q1.pdf))

What interests me about this map isn't the idea that the boundaries are clear-cut. It's
understanding why people can talk about “AI” while describing technologies and
experiences that have almost nothing in common.

## The developer's new role

If AI writes the code, the developer's work moves to two places.

**Before: the frame.** What the agent is allowed to do, where it looks for information,
how it should source it, what a clean result should look like. That's the harness, the
heart of human on the loop. A good harness looks like a good spec.

**After: discernment.** Reviewing not line by line, but the approach: does the analysis
hold up, is the chosen approach the right one, does it fit with the rest? My feedback to
Blue is about that, and rarely about a line of code.

**And during: production follow-up.** The work isn't over when the PR is merged. We have
to watch what happens with real users: is the service still responding quickly, are
errors rising, is the agent doing what we asked — and what is it costing? I prefer to
start with a small rollout, compare the signals with the previous version, then expand
if all looks good. If things go sideways, we need to be able to stop the agent or roll
back quickly. Feedback and incidents then become test cases, rules or improvements to
the harness. **We don't delegate responsibility when we delegate execution.**

Google describes this as canarying: gradually exposing a small portion of traffic to a
new version, comparing it with the stable version, and pausing or rolling back if the
signals worsen ([Google SRE Workbook](https://sre.google/workbook/canarying-releases/)).

## And it's not just about development

I'm convinced this move from in to on the loop isn't limited to developers. Let's take
three jobs.

![In marketing, sales and support, the agent prepares and executes repetitive tasks; the professional sets the rules, makes judgment calls, and keeps decisions that affect the brand, the relationship or the customer.](/images/articles/claude-duet-grok-bot-muse-agents-ia/roles-en.svg)

To make the difference concrete, keep the same task and change where the human sits in
the loop:

### Marketing: produce variations, keep the voice

Imagine a product launch. The agent can turn a headline into twenty variants, adapt the
formats for each social network and suggest visuals.

> “Prepare three versions for each channel. Don't invent numbers or testimonials. Flag
> any product claim that isn't in the brief.”

- **In the loop:** the marketer reads and edits every headline before it goes live.
- **On the loop:** they set the audience, tone and approved claims, then watch the
  results and step in if the agent goes off brief.
- **Outside the loop:** the agent picks variants and publishes them within a budget, with
  no approval before each post.

### Sales: automate follow-up, not trust

Before a meeting, an agent can summarize account history, prepare questions and update
the CRM.

- **In the loop:** the salesperson reviews every email, follow-up and important CRM
  update.
- **On the loop:** they define the right accounts, tone and follow-up rhythm; the agent
  handles routine follow-up and flags unexpected replies or high-stakes opportunities.
- **Outside the loop:** the agent picks prospects and runs the entire follow-up sequence
  without review. The salesperson looks at the results afterward.

And if the summary mixes up two customers? *On the loop*, the source should be visible
before the call, and the salesperson should be able to stop the sequence. The relationship
and negotiation still take human judgment.

### Support: fix the answer, then the cause

- **In the loop:** the support specialist reads and edits every answer before it is sent.
- **On the loop:** they maintain the knowledge base and escalation rules. The agent
  answers simple questions but routes unclear refunds, complaints and sensitive cases to
  a person.
- **Outside the loop:** the agent handles and closes every ticket without case-by-case
  review. The specialist only sees errors afterward — sometimes too late.

If the agent offers a refund the customer isn't entitled to, correcting that reply is *in
the loop*. Adding the missing rule and checking that it prevents the next mistake is *on
the loop*. **Fix the incident, then the system that made it possible.**

In all three cases, the job doesn't disappear: it moves up a level. Less time spent
executing, and much more time setting the frame and judging the result.

<mark>The valuable skill isn't only producing faster: it's knowing what to delegate,
what to check and when to take control back.</mark>

## What if we accepted silent errors?

I'm enthusiastic, but not naive. To me, we need two things: **a frame and a safety
net**. Without them, an agent can seek out and combine information far beyond what we
asked it to do.

And this isn't just theoretical:

- **OpenAI** decided not to release GPT-6.1 Astra, which its safety lead said did not
  stay sufficiently within its scope. The company also apologized after internal agents
  accessed nonpublic information on an Australian government site. ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/openai-launches-dots-ai-agents-safety-questions-rcna600338))
- **Muse and Amazon**: Amazon blocked the agent on its site, citing, among other things,
  that it browsed without identifying itself. ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/09/21/amazon-blocks-metas-muse-agent-from-shopping-on-users-behalf/))
- **Muse and privacy**: researchers found around 6,800 links to shared conversations;
  Meta suspended the feature on October 20. ([eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/news/meta-muse-chat-exposure/))

### The problem with errors we don't see

Even with a good frame, an error can slip through. Anthropic acknowledges, for example,
that Claude Duet [may misread a thread or misjudge urgency](https://claude.com/product/claude-duet).

A bug someone spots can be fixed. A false claim published without checking can be harder
to take back. **The stakes depend on the context**, and someone needs to be able to
look, understand and take control back.

> What if tomorrow we accepted these errors because the productivity gains were too
> tempting? One error we don't see, then another… **Could that be the beginning of a
> decline?**

I'm deliberately taking the question far: if we give AI more and more autonomy without
limits, who's to say we won't one day see the robots from *I, Robot* in the street?
That's a science-fiction image, not a prediction. But the question behind it feels real:
*how far are we willing to delegate, and who stays in control?*

## So, ready to move to “on the loop”?

No need to wait for Duet or Dots to arrive in France to change your method. If you want
an always-on agent right now,
[Hermes Agent](https://portal.nousresearch.com/cloud), from Nous Research, is open source
and self-hosted. But either way, the method matters more than the tool:

1. **Pick a repetitive, well-bounded task**, like a morning briefing or watching your
   PRs.
2. **Write the frame before launching anything**: what the agent can access, how it
   sources, what a good result looks like.
3. **Keep an approval step at the end**, like a draft PR that you review.
4. **Fix the harness, not the result.** When the agent gets it wrong, ask yourself what
   was missing from the frame.

Only then should you widen the scope.

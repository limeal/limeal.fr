In ten weeks, xAI, Meta, OpenAI and Anthropic all shipped the same thing: an always-on
AI agent that works in the background on its own computer and comes back to you when it
needs a decision. To me, this isn't a new chatbot feature, it's a real change of method:
we stop micromanaging AI, we give it a frame and we judge its work.

Let me say it right away: I haven't tested any of these products. What I have done is
build an agent at work that already does part of what they promise, and that's where I'm
writing from.

## From human in the loop to human on the loop

To place the human relative to agents, I'm using the three positions described by Kief
Morris (Thoughtworks) in
[Humans and Agents in Software Engineering Loops](https://www.martinfowler.com/articles/exploring-gen-ai/humans-and-agents.html),
published in March 2026 on Martin Fowler's site:

- **In the loop**: the human controls things inside the loop, for example by checking
  every generated line, and fixes the result directly. That's micromanagement.
- **On the loop**: the human builds and runs the loop, meaning the harness (specs,
  quality checks, guidance). When the result isn't right, they change the harness that
  produced it. As Morris puts it: "Rather than personally inspecting what the agents
  produce, we can make them better at producing it."
- **Outside the loop**: the human only keeps the "why" and leaves all of the "how" to
  the agents. That's vibe coding.

![Three diagrams: in the loop, the human checks every output of the agent; on the loop, they run the harness around the loop; outside the loop, they only set the goal](/images/articles/claude-duet-grok-bot-muse-agents-ia/loops.svg)

With the first assistants, we were in the loop. We split up the task, guided every step,
reread every line produced. AI was a tool we held by the hand.

With this year's models, we've entered the agentic era, and in the end almost every need
we had can be turned into an agent. The method changes completely:

1. we define the need together;
2. the developer sets the frame;
3. the AI does the rest;
4. the developer checks the result and gives feedback, no longer on the code, but ABOVE
   ALL on the thinking: the analysis and the implementation the AI chose.

That's the shift to human on the loop: the human no longer does everything themselves.
They build the frame, keep the decisions, and take back control at the moments that
matter.

The four products don't put the human in the same place, by the way. Claude Duet keeps
an approval step on sensitive actions (it asks before sending a message or paying).
Grok Bot goes much further: its agents split the work among themselves and only come to
you for judgment calls.

## Blue, my agent at work

I didn't wait for these products. At work, I use an agent I named Blue, which backs me
up on tickets every day. It's actually a whole team of sub-agents:

- **PR Babysitter** watches my pull requests: it checks the content, talks with
  CodeRabbit (the AI code review tool) and gives me its feedback;
- one agent takes simple tickets (and I do mean simple) and handles them from A to Z,
  all the way to opening a draft PR, which I review before anything else happens;
- one agent gives me a briefing in the morning, and another in the evening;
- one agent helps me write documentation, especially to explain the need in more detail;
- one agent pulls information from Slack when a message concerns me.

Let's be clear: Blue doesn't work in my place. Complex tickets, architecture and
decisions are still mine. Blue takes the repetitive work and the exploration, which frees
up time for what really takes thinking.

![Diagram of Blue: Paul sets the harness and reviews; Blue coordinates five sub-agents, PR Babysitter (which talks with CodeRabbit), Tickets, Briefing, Docs and Slack](/images/articles/claude-duet-grok-bot-muse-agents-ia/blue.svg)

Why Blue? Simply because I wanted a color name, and blue is the professional one. Its
personal counterpart, which I'm going to build, will be called Yellow.

I'll be honest: Blue wasn't good on the first try. I had to give it quite a lot of
feedback on how it worked, and what I refined wasn't Blue itself, it was its harness, in
other words the frame it works in. Once the harness is clean, the results follow.

When I started this article, I thought I was behind, still human in the loop. Rereading
these definitions, I realize I've already partly moved past it: when Blue gets it wrong,
I fix its harness, not its code. That's human on the loop, with a final approval step
I'm deliberately keeping (the draft PR).

The next step is the agent swarm: several agents that talk to each other, split the work
and only come back to the human to decide. Grok Bot already does it in group chats, and
all the big labs (Anthropic, OpenAI, xAI, Meta) now have their own always-on agent. It's
a vision I haven't fully grasped yet, and I fully intend to take a closer look, because
I'm convinced it's going to lead to something huge.

So, are we ready for a truly autonomous swarm? In my view, not yet, but we're getting
closer, and fast. An experiment published in October 2026 by Y Combinator with Olam Labs
illustrates this really well
([Agents in Games](https://labs.ycombinator.com/projects/games/index.html)). They set 50
agents loose in Minecraft for 3 hours, with a single goal: "Build a thriving
civilization". The result: around 5,900 messages exchanged, and a leader who emerged on
their own (Ada, who received 616 direct messages). Impressive! But the colony never
really thrived: 22 of the 34 deaths happened on the very first night.

And in the matches pitting a Claude team against a GPT team, you find exactly the flaws
I talk about below. The Claude team never changes its leader despite losing, it writes
its lessons into memory files… then repeats the same mistakes, and several of its agents
wrongly convince themselves that an action is impossible (where GPT finds a
workaround).

In short: the swarm already knows how to coordinate, but it doesn't yet know how to
question itself. And that's exactly where the human on the loop remains essential.

Today, I see three groups:

- a very small minority of people far ahead, already at the swarm stage, like the labs
  themselves;
- people like me, along with startups, SaaS companies and advanced tech teams, moving
  from human in the loop to human on the loop;
- and lots of developers still in the loop, whom I'm starting to see move with these new
  agents.

Everything is speeding up. Every year brings its own paradigm shift.

## The developer's new role

If AI writes the code, the developer's work moves to two places.

**Before: the frame.** What the agent is allowed to do, where it looks for information,
how it should source it, what a clean result should look like. That's the harness, the
heart of human on the loop. A good harness looks like a good spec.

**After: discernment.** Reviewing not line by line, but the approach: does the analysis
hold up, is the chosen approach the right one, does it fit with the rest? My feedback to
Blue is about that, and rarely about a line of code.

## And it's not just about development

I'm convinced this move from in to on the loop isn't limited to developers. Let's take
three jobs.

**Marketing.** An agent can turn a campaign into dozens of variants (hooks, visuals,
formats for each network). But the positioning, the audience and the brand's tone are set
by the marketer: that's their harness. And they're still the one who approves before
anything goes live, because a published mistake is a hit to the brand's image.

**Sales.** The agent can prepare meetings, follow up with prospects, keep the CRM up to
date. The salesperson decides who to target and how to talk to them, and keeps what can't
be delegated: the relationship, the negotiation, the trust. Nobody wants to sign with
someone who never really understood their problem.

**Customer support.** This may be the most telling example. When an agent gives a
customer a bad answer, fixing that particular answer is in the loop. Fixing the knowledge
base and the guidelines that produced it, so it doesn't happen again, is on the loop.
Exactly what I do with Blue's harness.

In all three cases, the job doesn't disappear: it moves up a level. Less time spent
executing, and much more time setting the frame and judging the result.

## Why I'm still wary

I'm enthusiastic, but not naive. My wariness comes down to two words: the frame and the
safety net.

**Without a frame, the agent overflows.** With a poorly built harness, an agent can go
searching far beyond its context, dig up information about someone's private life and
cross-reference it. If it isn't controlled and there's no safety net, things get
complicated fast.

And this isn't theoretical, not at all. The day before Dots launched, OpenAI decided not
to release a new model, GPT-6.1 Astra: according to its head of safety systems, it didn't
stay well enough within its scope and authorization. The same day, the company
apologized for an incident from June: internal agents, running a model that wasn't meant
to ship, had accessed nonpublic information on an Australian government website
([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/openai-launches-dots-ai-agents-safety-questions-rcna600338)).

On the privacy side, Muse has already had two alerts. Two weeks after its launch, Amazon
blocked it on its site, criticizing in particular that the agent browsed without
identifying itself
([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/09/21/amazon-blocks-metas-muse-agent-from-shopping-on-users-behalf/)).
Then in October, researchers found around 6,800 links to shared conversations, and Meta
suspended the feature on October 20
([eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/news/meta-muse-chat-exposure/)).

**Even with a frame, there will be silent errors.** Explaining clearly to the agent how
to source information and what a clean result should look like reduces the risk, but
doesn't remove it. The vendors admit it themselves: Anthropic writes that Claude Duet
[may misread a thread or miscalibrate what's urgent](https://claude.com/product/claude-duet).

These errors can be brought down to a minimal level, and in many fields, a small error
isn't a big deal. As a developer, what's a small error? It's a bug that someone notices
and fixes. In journalism, on the other hand, AI can't handle everything: facts will
always need checking.

Either way, you need an outside look at the analysis. Whether the human is *in* or *on*
the loop doesn't matter, as long as someone is there to check. The day there's no one
left, we enter a bubble where we accept silent errors.

## Where to start?

No need to wait for Duet or Dots to arrive in France to change your method. If you want
an always-on agent right now,
[Hermes Agent](https://portal.nousresearch.com/cloud), from Nous Research, is open source
and self-hosted. But either way, the method matters more than the tool:

1. **Pick a repetitive, well-bounded task**, like a morning briefing or watching your
   PRs.
2. **Write the frame before launching anything**: what the agent can access, how it
   sources, what a good result looks like.
3. **Keep an approval step at the end**, like a draft PR that you review.
4. **Fix the harness, not the result.** When the agent gets it wrong, ask yourself what
   was missing from the frame.

Only then should you widen the scope.
